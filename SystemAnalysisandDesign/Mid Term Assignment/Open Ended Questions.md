	a. Joint Application Development (JAD) and Rapid Application Development (RAD) represent significant advancements over traditional fact-finding methods in systems analysis and design. JAD is a structured, workshop-based approach that unites stakeholders, including business users, managers, and IT professionals, in intensive, facilitated sessions to define system requirements collaboratively. Rather than relying on an analyst to independently gather and interpret information, JAD promotes real-time consensus building, resolving conflicts and ambiguities on the spot, resulting in a mutually agreed-upon requirements document. RAD, by contrast, is a full iterative development methodology that prioritizes speed and user feedback through rapid prototyping cycles. It moves through requirements planning, user design, construction, and cutover phases within compressed timeboxes, allowing users to interact with working prototypes early and refine them continuously.
	 
	b. Traditional fact-finding methods, such as interviews, questionnaires, observation, and 
	document review, are largely analyst-driven, sequential, and passive, placing stakeholders in a reactive role. This creates a significant communication gap between user intent and system output, often resulting in costly errors discovered late in development. JAD addresses this by fostering collaborative, real-time dialogue among all parties, while RAD eliminates ambiguity by replacing written specifications with tangible, evolving prototypes. Together, JAD and RAD shift the development paradigm from documentation-heavy, linear processes to dynamic, user-centered methodologies that reduce misunderstandings, accelerate delivery timelines, and increase the likelihood of producing systems that genuinely meet organizational needs.
